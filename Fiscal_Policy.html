Possible Long-Run Supply-Side Effects

Glossary

Aggregate Demand (AD)

Total planned expenditure on domestic final goods and services at each general price level. AD = C + I + G + (X − M).

Aggregate Supply (AS)

Total real output producers are willing and able to supply at each general price level over a period of time. The Keynesian curve has horizontal, intermediate and vertical sections.

Macroeconomic Equilibrium

The intersection of AD and AS determines the equilibrium general price level and real national income.

Multiplier Effect

An autonomous spending change generates income, which leads to further rounds of induced consumption. Leakages through saving, taxation and imports limit successive rounds. A larger multiplier produces a larger total spending response for the same initial change.

No Further Rounds retains the initial spending effect. Small and Large add further rounds of induced consumption; these settings are illustrative, not numerical estimates. The equilibrium income change also depends on AS and changes in prices.

Initial Spending Change

The autonomous spending increase or decrease that starts the process. Its size is distinct from the size of the multiplier. The widget shows the combined AD shift, rather than animating individual multiplier rounds.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

Lower production costs increase SRAS, represented by a downwards shift. Higher costs decrease SRAS, represented by an upwards shift. This control holds productive capacity fixed.

Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)

Productive capacity determines full-employment output. An increase shifts LRAS rightwards; a decrease shifts it leftwards. This control holds the horizontal section's price level fixed.

Initial State of the Economy

On horizontal SRAS there is spare capacity. The intermediate section represents increasing capacity constraints. On vertical LRAS the economy is at full-employment output.

Fiscal Policy

Government spending and taxation can influence aggregate demand. This widget changes either government expenditure or taxes, holding the other instrument and other autonomous expenditure constant. The tax scenario represents personal and corporate tax changes together: disposable income affects C and after-tax profitability affects I.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy

An increase in government spending or a reduction in taxes raises AD and can generate further rounds of induced consumption.

Contractionary Fiscal Policy

A reduction in government spending or an increase in taxes lowers AD and can generate further reductions in induced consumption.

Policy Limitations

Outcomes depend on spare capacity, leakages, confidence, timing and implementation. Crowding out, financing constraints and automatic price-adjustment dynamics are not modelled. Long-run capacity effects are possible scenarios, not automatic consequences.

Taxes (T)

This scenario assumes personal and corporate taxes move together. Tax cuts raise disposable income and consumption (C), and raise after-tax profitability and investment (I); tax increases reverse these channels. The combined initial C/I response is smaller than an equally sized G change; only part of extra disposable income is spent and investment responsiveness is assumed. This is not a calibrated comparison of actual policies.
