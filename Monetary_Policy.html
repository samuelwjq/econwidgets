Glossary

Aggregate Demand (AD)

Total planned expenditure on domestic final goods and services at each general price level. AD = C + I + G + (X − M).

Aggregate Supply (AS)

Total real output producers are willing and able to supply at each general price level over a period of time. The Keynesian curve has horizontal, intermediate and vertical sections.

Macroeconomic Equilibrium

The intersection of AD and AS determines the equilibrium general price level and real national income.

Multiplier Effect

An autonomous spending change generates income, which leads to further rounds of induced consumption. Leakages through saving, taxation and imports limit successive rounds. A larger multiplier produces a larger total spending response for the same initial change.

No Further Rounds retains the initial spending effect. Small and Large add further rounds of induced consumption; these settings are illustrative, not numerical estimates. The equilibrium income change also depends on AS and changes in prices.

Initial Spending Change

The autonomous spending increase or decrease that starts the process. Its size is distinct from the size of the multiplier. The widget shows the combined AD shift, rather than animating individual multiplier rounds.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

Lower production costs increase SRAS, represented by a downwards shift. Higher costs decrease SRAS, represented by an upwards shift. This control holds productive capacity fixed.

Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)

Productive capacity determines full-employment output. An increase shifts LRAS rightwards; a decrease shifts it leftwards. This control holds the horizontal section's price level fixed.

Initial State of the Economy

On horizontal SRAS there is spare capacity. The intermediate section represents increasing capacity constraints. On vertical LRAS the economy is at full-employment output.

Monetary Policy

Interest-rate policy influences consumption and investment. Exchange-rate policy influences (X−M), with the direction depending on the Marshall–Lerner condition. The two instruments are explored separately.

Interest Rates

Lower interest rates can reduce borrowing costs and the incentive to save, raising consumption (C) and investment (I). Higher rates can reverse these effects. Responses depend on confidence and access to credit. The exchange-rate channel is excluded in interest-rate mode.

Exchange Rate

Depreciation makes domestic exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in domestic currency. Appreciation reverses these price changes. The impact on (X−M), measured in domestic currency, depends on demand responsiveness.

Marshall–Lerner Condition

Under the model's initial trade-balance assumption, depreciation improves (X−M) when the sum of the absolute price elasticities of demand for exports and imports exceeds one. Appreciation reverses this effect. When unchecked, this widget assumes the sum is below one, so these directions reverse. Equality, adjustment lags and the J-curve are not modelled.

Imported Input Costs

Appreciation can reduce the domestic-currency cost of imported inputs, increasing SRAS. Depreciation can raise these costs and decrease SRAS. The optional channel assumes some exchange-rate pass-through; its magnitude is illustrative.

Possible Long-Run Supply-Side Effects

Investment, technology and the cost of imported capital can influence productive capacity over time. The capacity slider permits either direction as an assumed scenario relative to the initial baseline. These effects are not automatic consequences of the selected policy.

Policy Limitations

The diagram is qualitative, with other influences held constant. It omits numerical policy calibration, adjustment lags, the J-curve, capital flows, feedback between interest and exchange rates, and automatic return to full employment. A fall in investment may slow capacity growth without reducing its absolute level.
